The millennium has opened with uncertainty and a sense of despair, as citizens' notions of justice come under heavy assault, and the framework of international and national relations crumbles in the face of an arrogance of power. Examples of how military power has trampled upon notions of democracy, of collective rights and citizens' well being, have been recorded over differences in time and space. To resist the injustice of military power has become a necessary burden for citizens.

In Bangladesh justice was not exacted from the Pakistan military and its collaborators, for the crimes of genocide and mass rape, committed in 1971. Not for lack of evidence. In fact over the years, Bangalis had painstakingly compiled evidence and much of this became the store of international knowledge on typology of military subjugation and brutality. First, the news filtered through the lens of foreign journalists, later after independence the government set up its own enquiries. Those held guilty of war crimes fell into two categories: the first included war criminals - 195 members of Pakistan military and bureaucracy - who were taken into Indian custody. The second included the local collaborators of the Pakistan military in “committing grave criminal offence and atrocities against the people of Bangladesh and to have cooperated with the then government in oppressing and obstructing the independence movement.” It was to ensure the legality of their trials that two renowned criminal lawyers had been asked to prepare a draft for the Bangladesh Collaborators’ (Special Tribunals) Order 1972. This law was later quoted as a standard setter for the International Criminal Court.

The country did not share the catharsis of witnessing exemplary trials of even the leading collaborators, as most of them were let off. This has had consequences for peace and security of ordinary citizens. As the collaborators merged into their communities, many quietly acquired influence or gained political power and became a threat to those who had survived their violence. The war criminals were released from Indian custody to Pakistan in 1974, following the Simla Agreement, on a commitment that they would be tried in Pakistan. This is where official interests or state denouement in Bangladesh did not coincide with popular demands for justice, for a symbolic atonement of the enormity of crimes committed against a civilian population. The families that had been affected, in village after village, had recognized and denounced the collaborators. But the wheels of popular claims to justice moved slowly; in the meantime other pressures built up; from families of Bangalis stranded in Pakistan, for whom repatriation of the living was a great priority; from international realpolitik. The Islamic States were keen to see Pakistan retain its armed strength. There is now ample evidence of Kissinger and Nixon’s support for army action and a US tilt towards Pakistan, as revealed in Christopher Hitchen’s book on *The Trial of Henry Kissinger*, and in Archer Blood’s recent publication, *The Cruel Birth of Bangladesh.*

Not only was this a moral wrong; absence of culpability had serious consequences for the militarization of both Bangladesh and Pakistan. The decision not to hold trials of the war criminals led, at the time, to considerable misgivings. Fakhruddin Ahmad, former Advisor to the Caretaker Government (1991), and at the time a member of the Foreign Service wrote thus: “ I believe that the most serious blunder on our part was not to start the trial of the Pakistani war criminals. The Nuremberg trials started immediately after the fall of Hitler. We could have at least tried Yahya and Tikka Khan in absentia for the murder of innocent people. I had suggested early action on the trial. I hinted that by holding a public trial of important Pakistan Generals we would be in a better bargaining position including repatriation (of Bangladeshis in Pakistan).” (See *Critical Times*, UPL, 1994, p 78).

Even though these crimes were not taken up either under international jurisdiction or under domestic legislation, the facts cannot be denied. In fact, contemporary foreign press reports and women’s testimonies of rape and widowhood form part of our oral history. In spite of Pakistan’s official silence, the Hamoodur Rahman Commission Report recorded that allegations against the military included “ raping of a large number of East Pakistani women by the officers and men of the Pakistan army as a deliberate act of revenge, retaliation and torture.”

Interests of real politik prevailed over the demands of justice. Bangladesh’s refusal to hold the perpetrators responsible has, over the years, subscribed to a simmering of frustration in citizens, for whom a denial of justice has also represented a recurrence of history. The demand for justice is not to be seen as an act of revenge alone. More to the point, it is a means to demand responsibility for war crimes, to prevent recurrence of military interventions. The failure to hold the military accountable for the genocide and mass rapes in Bangladesh has emboldened them to interfere repeatedly in civilian life in both Pakistan and Bangladesh. Where disputes between two contending communities demand a peaceful resolution, intervention by military action or non-constitutional means has led to long standing disruption of peace and security in the community.

* * *

Thirty two years later, apprehensions of intemperate, arbitrary military action remains a concern for conscientious citizens. Thus the enactment of a law to grant indemnity for incidents that occurred under Operation "Clean Heart" has been the source of much protest this year. Perhaps the military did act against some alleged criminals, under orders from the Cabinet, but that cannot be the main criteria for allowing them to defy the constitutional guarantee of freedom from torture and arbitrary arrest and detention. Newspaper reports suggested that 55 persons died as a result of Operation Clean Heart. Of them, 9 died in military custody, 8 after they were handed over to police custody and 38 of "heart attacks" following their interrogation in military custody, subsequent to torture, according to family sources or in trying to escape.

Some influential members of the government are reported to have questioned citizens’ protests, since the victims were alleged to be criminals. Alleged not proven, and killed by arbitrary decision, not by a court of law! Can we be sure that they were indeed criminals, since no legal process had been followed? And suppose they were criminals, should they be subject to arbitrary punishment, in which case what is the difference between a criminal killing a citizen and a member of the law enforcement personnel killing a citizen?

One hundred and ninety five Pakistani soldiers guilty of war crimes in 1971 did not face a trial. If they had, guilty individuals would have paid for their crimes against humanity. Instead the entire people of Pakistan have to take their guilt upon themselves. It is reported in the newspapers that two military personnel were tried by a military court in Dhaka for extortion during Operation "Clean Heart". This is a lesser crime than taking someone’s life, why is it then so improbable to demand justice in cases of arbitrary arrest or death under Operation "Clean Heart"?

One of the ministers in the present cabinet is reported to have said that he was proud to enact the Indemnity Act. As citizens we cannot be proud of any violation of the Constitution and any defiance of human rights. The government decided to lean upon the army to resolve a problem that was the making of political patronage. If powerful political leaders had not provided patronage, if the police had not been asked to refrain from law enforcement in individual cases, would these criminals have acquired immunity for their illegal actions. The government’s decision to give a blanket indemnity, and the parliament’s passive adoption of the Bill, will exonerate a few military personnel and in so doing will submit to the politics of armed power. But what becomes of justice for individual families of victims of Operation "Clean Heart" and indeed for a society that allows its law enforcement to be used a means of murder. There is not even an illusion of justice when a State refuses to take responsibility for crimes committed in its names, for reparations for war damages or compensation to the survivors.

Excesses of law enforcement have now been coined as ‘collateral damage’. But can such damage cover the enormity of genocide or of willfully contrived deaths. Pakistan expects us to forgive, but can forgiving be equated with forgetting! Genuine remorse would imply that the military itself should learn from its experience and not take over roles for which it has no aptitude. Each time it does so, it leaves the society in a worse shambles.

* * *

Assaults on a civil population are not particular to any place and time. They have become common weapons in military and para-military action across borders, within communities and within the political divide. Whether such crimes occurring in Palestine, Kashmir, Bangladesh, Chittagong Hill Tracts, Sri Lanka or elsewhere will ever be tried in international courts depends to some extent upon geo-political dynamics. Victors may not physically pay the price for their violations, but the brutality of each military intervention reminds us how our societies are torn apart, in the name of sovereignty, of law and order. The very framework for social co-existence has come under challenge.

And today, almost 60 years of a devastating world war, an entire international legal framework evolved over so many decades has come under challenge because of the naked might of military power. Even as the US led plans to execute ‘regime change’ or destroy WMD with missiles goes into operation, it has stirred protests across the world: protests against injustice, protests against hegemony and "empire", protests against violations of norms of international law. Protests have been voiced not only by ‘irresponsible street demonstrators’ or ‘extremists’ but also by a few conscientious members of the British government and some representatives in the US Congress. Many have spoken out against the hubris of the governments involved in publicly attacking the people of a country because the ruler was anathema to them.

Does this leave our claims for justice in tatters, and do we then succumb to the politics of domination? Or should we retort back to those who tell us that “if we are not with them we are terrorists” that we are neither one nor the other; that we will continue to believe in claims for justice and humanity. Because we have learnt over and over again, in our own domestic sphere, as well as in the international sphere, that peace and human security do not emerge from the barrel of a gun.
