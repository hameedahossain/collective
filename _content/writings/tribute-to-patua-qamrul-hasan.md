The arts of Bengal owe to a long standing tradition of folk lore and crafts. Two of our leading artists Shilpacharjya Zainul Abedin and Patua Qamrul Hasan paid tribute to this tradition, not only through their works of art but by inspiring institutional development of this rich tradition.

Soon after I came to Dhaka in the sixties I came to know both artists through their exhibits and through their inspiration of other artists and art institutions. They shared their love of crafts and taught us to appreciate artisans. I still possess a 200 year old kantha which Zainul Abedin gave me as a token of our friendship. At one of many exhibitions in Dhaka I chanced upon a painting by Qamrul Hasan. A dark blue canvas that over shadowed bulls in a storm. I immediately felt an attraction for it, and I have treasured it since in my home.

Qamrul Hasan used to paint in his own home but we often saw him in New Market, sitting outside a small textile shop called Rupayan. This belonged to his wife. He would often visit her and meet with other artist friends there. It became a popular meeting place.

Qamrul Hasan is well known for his work much of which draws upon folk designs and folk lore. But we remember him also as a politically conscious artist which is evident from his satirical sketches of Yahya Khan in 1970 and later autocrats of the eighties.

Many of us were inspired by him to form the National Crafts Council of Bangladesh in 1985. Amongst its members I would count on Professor Tofail Ahmed of the Tangail College, Mohammad Syedur Rahman of Bangla Academy, Dr Enamul Haque, the former director of the National Museum and Syeda Ruby Ghaznavi.

Of course we invited Qamrul Hasan to be the first president of the organization. Under his leadership we were able to organize a Crafts Exhibition in London in cooperation with the Commonwealth Foundation. Needless to say the exhibition was very successful and Qamrul Hasan contributed to its success by using the space for further artistry. The exhibition drew many visitors and many of them would discuss the art scene with Qamrul Hasan to find out about new developments in South Asia. He directed us to produce a catalogue for the exhibition with a cover that showed traditional clay dolls which used to be made by village women. He also wrote an illustrated article for the catalogue titled “Dolls and Toys” which illustrates how dolls were made and how they were used.

After his appointed as Chief Designer at BSCIC Qamrul Hasan set up training classes and encouraged women to use their natural talents. Many women attended his classes in making dolls and pottery items. He paid importance to using natural fibres and to protection of the environment. To prevent wastage he used old clothes and other used items. His leadership at BSCIC set a new trend in design based on folk traditions.

I served as Secretary of the Crafts Council when Qamrul Hasan was President. This was an educational opportunity for me. I used to visit him in his apartment in Monipuri para. I remember seeing a very large wooden trunk in his spacious room. He would ask me to open these and look through his rich collection of water colours. It was a delight to work with him as he led me into the many different stories of folk crafts and their creators. I feel sorry that we have not set up a museum or gallery in his name where his paintings could find a home.
