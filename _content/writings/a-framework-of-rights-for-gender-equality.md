As nations emerged from colonial binds the state has had to contend with diverse and conflicting demands from different communities, classes or groups of citizens to address the limitations on their freedom or to redress discrimination in social, political and economic relations.

In South Asian states the sources of gender inequality embedded in cultural traditions, customary practices and religious tenets have been reinforced by an hierarchical social order even after independence. In some states, these inequalities have been sustained in the Constitution or in national laws, while in others they persisted through social values.

On its independence Bangladesh adopted a framework of constitutional rights to equality for all citizens. However, Article 28 (1 and 2) of the Bangladesh Constitution limited equality to matters of the State and the public sphere only. While Article 28(4) recommended affirmative action for “backward legal classes, including women”, these guarantees do not extend to equal rights in marriage, divorce, guardianship or inheritance. Women in their personal lives remain subordinate to men

The Convention on Elimination of Discrimination against Women (1979) provided markers to measure progress in establishing equality of laws and policies. By ratifying this treaty in 1984 Bangladesh agreed to eliminate discriminatory laws and policies and to promote equal opportunities in development. The state committed itself to submit five yearly reports on its compliance with CEDAW. Bangladesh’s adoption of the Vienna Declaration and PoA (1995) has reinforced international markers for women’s human rights just as the Beijing Plan of Action (1995) and Sustainable Development Goals (SDG) are steps towards implementing a rights framework in its development policies and programs.

In the following pages, I will discuss how an international rights framework attempts to overcome gender discrimination and its instrumentality in promoting women’s rights to equality. The reports submitted by the government to the CEDAW Committee and to other treaty bodies, including the Universal Periodic Review assess the policies and programs that advance gender equality. These reports have been supplemented by alternative or shadow reports from citizens’ groups which highlight the constraints to equality. Responses by the CEDAW Committee or the Human Rights Council (in the case of the UPR Report) enable us to assess how far the State has moved towards the goals of gender equality.

### Compliance with CEDAW

Since Bangladesh submitted its initial report to the CEDAW Committee until its latest in 2016, (the Eighth Report), the state has maintained reservations to Articles 2 and 16© of the Convention on the grounds that “they conflict with Sharia law based on Holy Quran and Sunna.” The CEDAW Committee has held that these reservations are a constraint on the State’s full compliance with international standards for gender equality. After reviewing the issues raised in both the national and shadow reports, the Committee made several observations and recommendations for advancing gender equality. It urged the government to withdraw its reservations, in line with several other Muslim countries which have not maintained reservations and to reform national laws in conformity with Article 4 of CEDAW. It also recommended policies to eliminate inequality in access to education, employment and health. In 2018, the Committee questioned the measures taken to ensure security and justice for women survivors of violence under the Convention’s General Recommendation 33. It raised issues of inequality in the policy framework relating to the work place and access to health and questioned the continuing discrimination against women of different ethnicities or disabilities.

The Government’s report acknowledged that recent legislation had sought to prevent and protect women from violence (both domestic and other forms) and recent policies (such as the Multi Sector Program) aimed to raise awareness and mobilize public activism.

### UPR Reports

In meeting its reporting commitments, the government has also submitted consolidated reports to the Human Rights Council under the Universal Periodic Review (UPR) on compliance with universal human rights. This includes progress made in women’s rights. Bangladesh submitted its last report in the Third Cycle in 2018, in which it noted a reduction in mortality rates for women and children, and improvement in the former’s participation in education, health and employment. It mentioned adoption of the Child Marriage Restraint Act, 2017, and The Overseas Employment and Migrants Act, 2013, both of which concern women. The former was critiqued for permitting under age marriage, with parental consent. A National Action Plan to Prevent Violence against Women and Children (2013–2025) has set up legal facilities for protection and rehabilitation of women survivors of violence. Domestic Workers' Protection and Welfare Policy (2015) was adopted with a view to prevent abuse of domestic workers. However, in the absence of legislation, these policy interventions nave yet to lead to legal protection.

### UN Rapporteur on Violence against Women

The third source for assessing women’s rights is to be found in the Report of the Special Rapporteur on Violence against Women (2013). She examined its causes, consequences, and implications for the effective exercise and enjoyment of human rights by women. The report discussed the State’s responses, through the lens of the principle of due diligence, and analyzed the underlying social, political and economic causes of inequality which leads to violence.

She attributed the prevalence of a high degree of gender violence to Inequality and power imbalances between men and women. The persistence of patriarchal attitudes make for their subordinate roles and responsibilities. She analyzed how their unequal relations allowed the prevalence of domestic or dowry related violence, of child marriage, and community instigated violence. Justice evades women in both the traditional system of *shalish* (mediation) or sometimes in the judicial system. Women of ethnic minorities, Dalit women and those with different abilities were even more marginalized.

### Translating international standards into policy and action

The Bangladesh Government has set up mechanisms to promote women’s participation in development based on the Beijing Plan of Action. Subsequent evaluation studies on the actualization of the MDGs and SDGs suggest that planning may have led to a degree of improvement in life expectancy, mortality rates, parity up to secondary education and an increase in labour force participation, although on unequal terms of employment. The citizenship law has allowed a woman to pass on her citizenship rights to her foreign spouse.

Actualization of gender equality in different spheres of a woman’s life is subject to many challenges from a patriarchal social system to corporatization of the economy and political authoritarianism. A woman is subject to legal inequality in personal laws, which are based on her religion or on customary practice; in export industries a large number of women are employed at unequal wages at lower grades of work; in agriculture or in domestic work they have no legal protection. Affirmative action has increased women’s participation in political institutions and public services but it is comparatively lower than that of men.

The challenges to compliance with international standards of gender equality have been attributed to social and institutional causes. Thus structural conditions of patriarchal power relations and archaic legal provisions continue to obstruct the goals of gender equality.

These challenges have been recognized by the Government in its Eighth Report to CEDAW thus:

“The Government is also aware of its unfinished agenda. Some of the important areas like women’s low labour force participation, existence of violence against women, wage discrimination against women, inadequate presence of women in decision making positions need special attention. The progressive legal provisions protecting rights of women need enforcement. The Government takes the challenge of implementation of CEDAW and enforcement of other gender related laws to protect women’s rights and eliminate all discriminations.”

To overcome these challenges Bangladesh is reported to have included measures in the Seventh Five Year Plan in keeping with Goal Five of the SDGs. These policies will need to be monitored by women activists to ensure their effective implementation. And to ensure that the benefits reach women of different communities, ethnicities and classes.

Concerted pressure by a significant mass and systematic advocacy by informed groups may bend the government towards making efforts to implement the state’s commitment to equality and non-discrimination. Observations or recommendations by the CEDAW Committee and the Special Rapporteur offer us guiding steps towards more egalitarian policies and laws. Links with transnational feminist movements can act as further pressure points to activate change.

In striving for equality and rights women’s movements, representing diverse constituencies, have located their struggle in several sites, from the legal to the economic and political, from recognition of international standards to their realization on the ground. In pursuing Bangladesh’s international commitments to gender equality women’s movements need to follow several strategies from monitoring, implementation, reporting to international and national forums so that we move beyond rhetoric to reality.

Human rights and women’s rights activists can facilitate this by taking on more sustained tasks of disseminating awareness of women’s rights under CEDAW, monitoring and lobbying for government compliance with its provisions, using CEDAW articles to uphold women’s rights in court, and researching into law reforms. This is a struggle for justice that women across the world are engaged in.
