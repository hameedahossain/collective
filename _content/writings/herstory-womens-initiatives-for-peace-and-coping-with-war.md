The idea of reliving ‘Herstory’ of women’s initiatives for peace and our coping with the impact of wars was suggested by Nighat Khan as a start to this conference. We acknowledged that women’s actions for peace did not start in the international arena. We needed to go back to our own past to trace how in different ways, women in their own local communities, in border conflicts, or in social disruptions within borders were the precursors to Resolution 1325 and Resolution 1820. Women’s participation in the Gender Caucus for the International Criminal Court was a cumulative outcome of an accountability process we had wanted in our own region.

I will therefore take you back in history, to where I came from. This remembering and recognition of our past is very important because it is the starting point for the work of international agencies or regional organizations. It is important to see where women were placed, what they were doing, in the South Asia region, which as we know, or are experiencing today, is a site for continuing conflicts, confrontations and strife. Many of these conflicts are related to identity differences - of ethnicity, religion, caste – they are expressed in communal violence, in wars between India and Pakistan, the crimes against humanity during the war of independence in Bangladesh, and the continuum of state violence on our borders and also within borders.

What has been the response from women’s groups and, not only women’s groups, but also other human rights and politically active groups? Has it represented a popular resistance to militarization, domination by militaries and a militaristic approach to governance in South Asia? We have supported our governments in promoting international peace efforts and acclaimed resolutions taken in SAARC on women’s role in peace and conflict.

In tracing the strands of women’s peace efforts to the past let me take you back to say, as far back as 1947. Let us look through a micro analysis of what the women’s responses have been and compare it with a macro analysis of positions taken by the state. The tragic divisions in 1947 have been drawn by Khushwant Singh in *Train to Pakistan -* graphic descriptions of train loads of injured women, men and children streaming in for shelter as communal violence between Hindus and Muslims destroyed the social fabric.

Even as governments and militaries celebrated the formation of two independent states. women were on the side lines but expressing concern at the violence consequences of partition? I remember in my own hometown of Hyderabad Sindh, my grandmother, 50 year old woman with no formal education, took over the healing process. She went into an abandoned house, turned it overnight into a hospital and recruited all her children and neighbours into providing services for many women, survivors of sexual and other forms of physical and mental violence, who arrived from across the new border. Even I was recruited to nursing duties. I was eleven years old and had little knowledge of what to do, except for a first aid course I had taken in school. Similar experiences of women responding to the crises in each community, reaching out to support women who had suffered sexual violence, need retelling.

In East Bengal (then a part of Pakistan, now Bangladesh) at the time, there were community organizations, women’s *shamitis*, as they were known, formed across the religious divide. Both Hindu and Muslim women worked together. The Muslim women supported and protected Hindu women who were likely to be targeted by communal gangs. Sufia Kamal who had crossed over from Kolkota joined hands with Leela Nag to protect women living in vulnerable areas of the city, and their *shamiti* took the lead in promoting communal harmony. Sadly, Leela Nag who had started a school for girls left perforce because of communal outbreaks. In West Bengal it was the other way round, with Hindu women protecting Muslims.

What was the macro picture of the state response? If you have read Kamla Bhasin and Ritu Menon’s *Borders and Boundaries* or Urvashi Butalia’s *The Other Side of Silence*, you will know how insensitive state machineries were in dealing with the humanitarian crises. The Indian government sponsored committees to identify abducted women and many of them were forcibly sent back to where they had come from because of the religion of the majority of that community was the same as that of the abducted woman. This was done arbitrarily often without the woman’s consent. There was this compulsion from the state whereas the women’s response was much more caring and supportive of the survivors or those who were lost or taken away from their homes.

Communal outbursts persist in all our countries and we cannot seem to settle down and live together to respect and tolerate diversity. So in Gujarat in 2002, as we know, there was terrible violence against the Muslims. In Bangladesh in the elections of 2001, and just after, there were reports of brutal attacks on the Hindus. There were stories of rape in both cases. Here again, you find the women’s response not only trying to support and care for them but also taking up legal challenges, particularly in cases of rape. The cases in Gujarat have been well documented, in Bangladesh too, several human rights and women’s organizations have filed court cases.

Then we come to 1971, the war of independence in Bangladesh, where an elected Constituent Assembly (at that time Bangladesh was a part of Pakistan) was rejected by the Pakistan military. On 25 March the military started their armed attack in different areas of the city; during nine months they committed crimes against humanity, until the troops surrendered on 16 December and Bangladesh became independent.

Within Bangladesh women suffered brutal forms of violence, physical, sexual and psychological. But they also resisted by sheltering freedom fighters, carrying messages, hiding arms and so on. They became the peace keepers looking after and protecting their families. After the war again we find women working together not only with the survivors but also taking up a legal challenge, demanding that sexual violence be recognized by the state, that women who suffered the violence be given a space to share their experiences.

What was the state response at that time? Their primary concern was to provide shelter and find means of economic rehabilitation of the women who had suddenly found themselves widowed without the male crutch they were used to. In the rehabilitation schemes women were given vocational training and provided job quotas in public service.

But there was a silence on the issue of rape and the social stigma remained. Therefore, within each community the women did not find the space to share their experiences and to build solidarity amongst themselves. The women who had conceived as a result of rape by the Pakistani soldiers or local collaborators were made to undergo abortions; some of the women who had already given birth to babies, gave them up for adoption. We can see two different types of response. The state took on a social welfare approach to deal with their economic survival, but did little to overcome the social rejection to work towards a social transformation. Many women engaged in social welfare as well, but they also tried to raise the issue of women in war. Two women members of Parliament persuaded the Prime Minister to recognize the women survivors of rape as *Birangonas (*literally brave women, but the name was misused to imply fallen women).

At the time, in 1973, Bangladesh enacted a law for the trial for war crime that included rape. This preceded the incorporation of rape in the International Criminal Court statutes. Over the last forty years or so, the struggle for justice for war crimes, particularly for sexual crimes has been sustained through various forms of documentation of oral histories of women where victims have talked about themselves and what happened in those nine months. A university teacher published the first book entitled *Ami Birangana Bolchi*, after collecting testimonies from seven rape survivors. This was followed by other books, media projections and films.

It was interesting that a very strong movement for the trial of crimes against humanity initiated by two women Sufia Kamal and Jahanara Imam led to a People’s Tribunal which was held in Dhaka in 1992/93. Three women came from Kushtia to testify at the Tribunal. But before they could do so the Government clamped down on the proceedings and started sedition trials against the organisers.

The struggle for accountability for war crimes has continued, and within this women have been active in pressing for a trial of sexual violence, leading to retributive and restorative justice. In 2010, the Government set up war crime tribunals in which 12 have been charged with committing and abetting in war crimes, and by 2012, six have been sentenced to death penalty. Charges of rape were also included and arrangements made for testimonies in camera, but victims were unable to testify as they were no longer in Bangladesh.

So, this is what happened in Bangladesh, with women’s groups coming together to create a space for women survivors to talk of their experiences of violence and their strategies for survival. Their oral histories have been published and testimonies filmed as a part of remembering. During this period women have reached across our borders. They have held hands and listened across national boundaries and worked together. We find that in 1971, for instance, Kumari Jayawardena I remember was demonstrating outside the Pakistani embassy in Colombo; in Pakistan itself even though it was a tough time with the Martial Law Government arresting people for speaking out, (Hina Jilani’s father was arrested because he questioned General Yahya Khan about Sheikh Mujibur Rehman’s arrest and so on 40 persons, including Tahira Mazar Ali Khan a trade union leader, issued a public statement in August or September. Nighat Said Khan was also a part of this group. It was a very brave thing to do. As the refugees went from Bangladesh into West Bengal, it was women in West Bengal who received them.

Much later, in the 1980s as we began to meet at various meetings, women from all three countries spoke out against war. When Nighat came to Dhaka for a meeting she said very clearly and this may not be popular with the Pakistan Government even today, but she said, “The Government of Pakistan must apologize and apology is not just saying sorry, it is much more than that.” She was probably the first who came to Bangladesh and said that. Subsequently in 1990s we met at a feminist meeting in Bangalore organised by Kamla Bhasin. Nighat and Roshmi and many of us were there, including women from Sri Lanka.

Once again we spoke of the destructive consequences of conflict in South Asia, and how governments and militaries had created hostilities to keep people apart. We discussed our efforts to resist militarization and use of arms but also how to positively work towards peace. We drafted a very significant declaration which articulated the feminist voice.

Subsequently, at Beijing, I was not there personally, but I heard from others, who were, that the Pakistani women’s group at the NGO forum got together with the Bangladeshi group and apologized for 1971. I do not think a similar apology came up at the official conference. When the press put this question to Benazir Bhutto who was then the Prime Minister she claimed to be unaware of what had happened.

In all our countries the military has perpetuated hostility amongst the South Asian states, and in some cases it has been the source of violence within each state. Women’s activism in South Asia has therefore identified how militarisation, identity politics has contributed to conflicts. At a meeting in Colombo, organized by Radhika Coomeraswami many of us discussed our strategies for promoting peace and ending conflict. A most important event was the Tokyo Tribunal in 2000. A central focus of the conference was to demand an apology from the Japanese Government for the crimes committed against Comfort Women. Even though the conference was being held 50-60 years later, the women found courage to talk about what had happened.

Women from other countries also came to the Tokyo Tribunal. From Bangladesh, the first women who spoke about the rape and sexual violence inflicted on her in 1971 came with us to the Tokyo Conference and I was with her. She spoke out. What she told me after was that the solidarity amongst women and those who believed in peace to resist violence and particularly state violence, made her determined to carry on demanding an end to state violence. Many of these regional meetings contributed to the international movements for peace. Rhonda Copelin in the United States was working towards an international framework for war crimes and crimes against women. These initiatives culminated in the formation of the Gender Caucus at the International Criminal Court.
