South Asian Women's Fund: Tenth Anniversary Presentation

Marks SAWF's ten years of funding grassroots women's organisations and places it within South Asian women's movements, from Roqaiya Sakhawat onwards. It reviews gains (domestic violence laws, sexual harassment guidelines, recognition of wartime rape) and the continuing obstacles of patriarchy, identity politics and conflict. It closes with a commitment to democracy, non-violence and human rights.

By Hameeda Hossain25 March 2016

South Asian Women's Fund (SAWF) members' consultation, Kathmandu.

Thank you for inviting me to join this special gathering that marks the tenth year of SAWF’s journey – a journey that has been dedicated to supporting women’s movements at the grass roots, and to be a part of the struggle. SAWF’s primary objective was to give financial support to grass roots women’s organisations in the region. But its role is not that of an external donor. In fact it has played a leading role in promoting gender and social justice, within a framework of human rights.

Let us acknowledge the contribution made by SAWF in reaching out to women whose struggles would otherwise have remained invisible. SAWF’s experience over ten years, as well as its consultations in the region, has enhanced the struggle for women’s rights as human rights. In particular it has reached out to a growing number of small, frontline organisations, in defending freedom of movement, within the country and across borders, and promoting justice in women’s personal and public lives. It has supported feminist ideas and practices and encouraged young women leaders to work for a gender and socially just society.

I would like us today to recognise SAWF’s contributions as part of the women’s movements for fundamental change: a sustained struggle for transforming social norms and state structures by promoting equality of all citizens, irrespective of our genders, ethnicity, religion, caste and class. We have collectively worked for a society that will encourage and promote freedom of expression, thought and belief.

We should assess therefore where we have reached in our struggles for gender and social justice, and what challenges lie ahead. We should not forget that at each stage of our struggle we have been confronted with patriarchal resistance that seeks to silence us or to deflect the direction of our movements.

The beginnings of the women’s movements in South Asia can be traced to our nationalist struggles, if not earlier. Going back in history, we find in Bangladesh, in the early twentieth century, Roqaiya Sakhawat campaigned for Muslim women’s education, and spoke out against purdah; in India Sarojini Naidu, a Congress leader demanded education; these women were followed by Kumari Jayawardene in Sri Lanka or Ismat Chughtai who wrote about sexual freedom.

The post independence nationalist reality, however, did not match with women’s notions of freedom. In each state, communal and identity politics has shaped our personal lives and created divisions based on religion, ethnicity, caste or language. Our multiple identities have made us targets of different discriminatory laws and practices.

In each of our countries we have struggled against subordination within the family, inequality at the work place and invisibility in state structures. We have turned to the state for reforms in personal laws to free us from traditional patriarchal norms and customs; to enact labour laws that would ensure equal wages for equal work and safety and health at the work place; we have demanded representation in state institutions so that our voices could be heard.

We have made some gains in South Asia. Most of our parliaments have enacted legislation on domestic violence that penalizes the perpetrator and protects the victim; court decisions have pronounced guidelines for action against sexual harassment. Maternity leave and crèches are being introduced slowly in the public sector. Reservations through elections has created a limited space for women in parliament or other representative institutions. UN surveys show that state policies have also contributed to some improvement in statistics for maternal mortality, life expectancy or education and health.

Women have worked for peace across borders and an end to hostility in the region. In spite of our common histories and common cultures, societies in South Asia are divisive. In the political conflicts in 1971 in Bangladesh, in Baluchistan, in Kashmir and more recently in Sri Lanka women have been targets of violence perpetrated by the state and non state actors. It was through a concerted campaign within the region that rape was finally recognized as a war crime in the Rome Statutes. In Bangladesh women survivors of rape have been recognized as freedom fighters and were able to give evidence at the Tribunal. Similar initiatives are being taken in Sri Lanka. It is to be hoped that justice will prevail in conflict zones in India and Pakistan as well.

We seek a fundamental change in our social, political and economic conditions. Hostility amongst countries of South Asia and divisions within each country, based on ethnicity, religion and caste, which create inequalities.

As feminist activists, as human rights defenders we have been opposed to economic domination, political authoritarianism and religious extremism. Our commitment has been to democratic structures in which we can participate freely, to a consultative decision making and to non-violent and negotiated processes of conflict resolution. As feminist activists we must ensure that the principles of human rights, equality, non-discrimination and human dignity are central to all economic, political and social development.

This consultation of SAWF members is very important because it opens a process of exchanging experiences in defending women’s rights, in providing protection and legal support whether it is women caught in family disputes, as workers in export industries or caught in the process of trafficking and migration. It is from our experiences of working at the grass roots that we can assess what actions and strategies we need to adopt to meet our goals of equality and justice, human rights and dignity.
