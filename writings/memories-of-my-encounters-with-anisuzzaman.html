Memories of My Encounters with Anisuzzaman

By Hameeda Hossain

In the sixties the teachers’ room at the University of Dhaka was the hub of intellectual exchanges on current affairs, history and politics. Discussions were rife around the subject of Bengal – its contemporary discomforts and political dissent, its cultural and literary revival. Professor Razzaque used to be the centre of attention in the teachers’ room, drawing young teachers and often students around him. This is where I was introduced to Bangladesh and to a stream of scholars who were then stimulating political and intellectual ideas of political and cultural transformation.

The sixties were a dynamic period in Bangladesh’s history with economists raising issues of disparity, political scientists examining how centralized power alienated state from citizens; literary critiques of contemporary writings were being published in contemporary journals. Whether it was at Professor Razaque’s house, at the Teachers’ room or Teachers’ Club, Anis was an integral part of these lively discussions. This is where I first met him and heard him contribute to the discussions. His voice had a calming effect on the emotionally charged discussions, particularly as he drew upon a deep knowledge of literature, particularly Bangla literature,

A year or two later, Anis became my Bangla teacher. Soon after I arrived in Dhaka in 1964 I realized that I couldn’t get too far unless I could understand and speak in Bangla. Otherwise I would have to remain silent. Along with Salma Sobhan I decided to enrol in a six month course for beginners at the Bangla Academy. We were fortunate in having three famous teachers, who were not only proficient in the language but equally so in the literature. We listened raptly to Dr Munir Choudhury, a well known playwright read to us. Dr Mufazzal Haider Chowdhury was a disciplinarian about usage. Dr Anisuzaman introduced us to Rabindranath’s Cheler Bela, a delightful expose of boyhood, made even more interesting by the way Anis taught it, reading from contemporary texts, with comparisons from other languages.

Learning was a pleasure without pain because there were no exams at the end of the day. On the contrary, Anis had to submit to students’ questions. One question must have been particularly embarrassing. In our class there were six Chinese students from the People’s Republic of China, who had been sent out by their government to study the language. They did so with great perseverance, reading only Bangla newspapers and speaking to all in only Bangla. They must have been model students but they put Anis’ pedagogy to the test when one of them stood up and asked: “Sir, how do you translate ‘licking dogs of imperialism’ into Bangla.” This was followed by a lengthy discussion on political ideologies which Anis had to moderate. I do not remember if the class was given a literal or metaphorical translation. Nor do I know if the curious student ever used the expression, but most of us were impressed by Anis’ response.

I had been very actively engaged with the weaving communities around Dhaka, learning about how their production had survived the pressures of the marketing systems. Professor Razzaque had interested me in pursuing my academic interest in history. An opportunity arose to look back in history and study textile production in Bengal when I went to Oxford in 1975. As I started work on my thesis I had to study manuscripts of the East India Company in the India Office Library in London. On my first day the librarian informed me of several Bangla manuscripts which had been found recently and were awaiting a scholar from Bangladesh to translate these. To my surprise the scholar who took up the challenge was no other than Professor Anisuzaman. I spotted him pouring over several large, heavily bound manuscripts. They included correspondence between the Factory Resident of the East India Company stationed in Dhaka and the Company’s gomashtas in eight Dhaka arangs, account books of payments and orders given to weavers in the arangs, complaints from weavers and other matters.

For a scholar of Rabindranath Tagore, who had worked on the twentieth century, hand written eighteenth century letters and accounts must have presented a challenge. It required painstaking labour , nevertheless, within several months Anis was able to translate these into English. His manuscript published in 1981 under the title of Factory Records and other Bengali Correspondence in the India Office and Records has no doubt been of great value to scholars of Indian economic and social history. For me his translations were invaluable as these sources provided rare insights into the world of the weavers of muslin and jamdani around Dhaka. I was very grateful to Anis, for even as he sat translating page by page, I was able to use the manuscripts, then catalogued as Bengal MS, to develop my thesis on the exploitative system of production created by European companies. His literary acumen added to a new chapter of history.
