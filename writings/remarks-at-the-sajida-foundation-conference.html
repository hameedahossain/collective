Remarks at the SAJIDA Foundation Conference

Dr. Hossain draws three lessons from founding and running ASK with Salma Sobhan: rights matter, institutions grow by adapting to people's realities, and law shapes how people claim justice. She illustrates them with ASK's outreach, paralegals, fact-finding, shelter, counselling, the 'Jokhon Tokhon' drop-in school for working children, and its films and publications. She concludes that a good institution must keep learning.

By Hameeda Hossain17 September 2026

Syed Humayun Kabir Memorial Research Symposium (SAJIDA Foundation & Banglar Pathshala Foundation), Dhaka Sheraton. Read the original ↗

When I think about what I have learnt from Ain o Salish Kendra, or ASK, there are three main lessons I’d like to share with you.

The first is about the importance of rights.

The second is that an institution does not begin with a fixed idea of what it is going to be. It develops by adapting to peoples’ lived realities.

And the third lesson is that the law matters because it shapes how people understand their rights and obligations, and how they make claims for justice.

I will try to illustrate these lessons from my experience in setting up and working at ASK.

ASK was set up when Salma Sobhan and I came back to Bangladesh in the 1980s after living in exile in Oxford since 1975. We wanted to create something practical, particularly for women and children, where they could get help to resolve disputes.

At that time, many organisations were working on women’s income and livelihoods, but less attention was being given to women’s rights and to issues of justice. So we set up ASK as a law and mediation centre, to defend rights and to resolve disputes.

In the beginning, we sat in our office, and waited for people to come to us for legal aid. But of course no-one came!

So we learned a lesson. It was not enough to say, “We are here. Come and ask for your rights.”

People often didn’t know they had rights or thow to get a remedy. They told us their husband beats them, or their mother-in-law had taken their property. They understood clearly this was wrong and were suffering, but they did not know what they could do.

So we realised the importance of first raising awareness about rights. This was an important lessons for us: innovation often comes from paying attention to what is not working.

Also, instead of waiting for people to come to us, we went to them. When we became involved in the Taltola slum eviction, for example, lawyers could fight the case in court, but families also needed support. Fieldworkers from ASK would go to them, document what happened, collect their evidence, and understand what they needed.

That led us to develop paralegals and fact-finding work. We recruited young lawyers and workers from the workers’ federation, so they could talk to people, keep track of what was happening and alert us to cases that needed attention.

Every morning, we read the newspapers to identify issues that needed investigation. We also went to places such as prisons and garment factories to document conditions. That’s how our research and documentation department started.

We also learned that winning a case was of little use if a woman remained in an unsafe environment while waiting for her case to be resolved.

So ASK opened its own shelter home, where a woman could stay safely and be informed about the progress of her case.

A woman might also need counselling, training, or a way of earning an income before she had the confidence to pursue her rights. So, from 1996, we invited skilled counsellors from India and Australia to run counselling courses in Dhaka, not only for ASK staff but also for other organisations. We learnt that counselling was essential in building trust.

Innovation also meant resisting solutions that looked correct but could cause harm. Let me give you an example. In 1993, when the US proposed the Harkins Bill demanding a ban on child labour, our position was that in the absence of an effective policy, working children would continue to work in even more exploitative conditions.

So,instead of simply supporting a ban, we ran a flexible drop-in school, “Jokhon Tokhon,” where working children could come at any time to study. We also campaigned for similar opportunities for domestic workers.

ASK did not confine itself to legal work. It worked through art, theatre and film. We produced a documentary on the politics of so called “fatwa” violence and published a collection of Birangonas’ testimonies of their experiences of sexual violence during the Liberation War. Our annual human rights report and our analysis of the legal situation became sources for researchers, activists and legal professionals.

What I have learnt is that collaboration is of utmost importance. There have been many challenges, but progress was possible because people worked together, fighting for everyone’s rights, regardless of their beliefs or affiliations.

Finally, none of these innovations were innovations for their own sake. They came from encountering a problem and asking, “What else is needed here?”

That is probably my biggest learning about institutions. A good institution must be able to learn. It cannot become so attached to its original structure that it stops responding to reality.

I hope this is helpful for today’s discussion.
