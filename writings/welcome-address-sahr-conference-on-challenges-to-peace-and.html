Welcome Address: SAHR Conference on Challenges to Peace and Prospects of Cooperation in South Asia

Dr. Hossain's welcome speech for South Asians for Human Rights (SAHR). It expresses solidarity with Pakistan's flood victims and argues that South Asia is at a crossroads between economic promise and poverty, conflict, extremism and state impunity. It introduces SAHR's history since the Neemrana Declaration and its work on minorities and lawless law enforcement, and urges citizens to press SAARC governments to act.

By Hameeda Hossain13–15 September 2010

SAHR regional conference “Challenges to Peace and Prospects for Cooperation in South Asia”, India International Centre, New Delhi. Read the original ↗

Let me extend a warm welcome to all of you for joining SAHR’s conference on Challenges to Peace and Prospects of Cooperation in South Asia. I would particularly like to thank those of us coming from neighbouring countries, for their patience and perseverance in facing immigration hurdles.

We had hoped that Mr Gujral, SAHR’s chairperson would be able to inaugurate this meeting, but he has regretted he cannot join us due to his health. He has very kindly sent a message for the success of the conference.

On behalf of all of us I would like to express our sincere sympathies and solidarity with the people of Pakistan who have faced unprecedented devastation, caused initially by natural flooding, but according to reports, which may have been exacerbated by a mess up of the irrigation system. Visible changes in the environment have compelled our states to wake up to the need to address the impact of climate change. But we still ignore how some of our economic policies may destroy our environment and our livelihoods. We have talked of eliminating poverty but have allowed an imbalanced development that has disabled our capacities to overcome economic want, political instability and social unrest.

Today, South Asia stands at a critical cross roads. On the one hand, the region is praised by international experts for its economic potential; on the other, our countries are mired in poverty, hostage to long standing disputes and hostility, our societies are exposed to communal violence and extremist militancy. Counter terrorist strategies by the state forces have allowed impunity that has further generated insecurity amongst the people. .

At this critical moment South Asian states desperately need to settle disputes, to implement agreements and to lay a framework for cooperation and for an equitable and fair sharing of resources. It is important for citizens to act, to speak out and to insist that our governments – political leaders, bureaucracy and militaries – work towards building just societies. While citizens have celebrated the end of military rule, and the revival of representative systems of governance, our celebrations have been tempered by our experience of the decline of democracies into populist authoritarianism and patronage, which constrains citizens’ participation in decision making.

SAHR supports the strategies proposed by many human rights defenders and political activists. These include proposals for a South Asian Union, of connectivity, of trade exchanges, interaction between peoples and governments of South Asia. Even SAARC has reiterated in its declaration issued at the 16th Summit in Thimpu, its commitments to economic and environmental security, promoting a common approach to connectivity, energy resources and climate change. There was talk of making functional the SAARC Social Charter and SAFTA, and to formulating a Democracy Charter. It is ironical, however, that last week, a meeting of government functionaries held in Dhaka formulated a draft of such a charter, but without the involvement of citizens. It is time for citizens to penetrate this bamboo curtain, and to make SAARC governments act upon their words.

Some of you have been associated with SAHR. For others, let me give a brief introduction. South Asians for Human Rights came into formal existence in 2001 at Neemrana. But the ideas behind the need for a regional approach to promoting and protecting human rights had germinated during many consultations and shared activities amongst human rights defenders from the region.

The Neemrana Declaration by SAHR acknowledged that “human rights violations in one country often spills over into the other countries in the region and potentially generates bilateral/regional tensions and hostility, thus threatening peace”.

Ten years on SAHR continues to raise concern at the persistence of parochial, patriarchal and militarist policies which have contributed to communal, ethnic, gender based discrimination and violence, within states and disputes between states. The recent emergence of religious extremism and militancy has fed on conflicts and communalization in South Asian countries. In 2007, the South Asian Commission on Rights of Minorities, formed by SAHR revealed how laws and policies perpetuated discrimination against minorities and led to violence. More recent investigative reports by SAHR point to the communalization of educational texts, media and administrative measures. Equally disturbing are the methods of lawless law enforcement used by the state. A four country study conducted for SAHR provided testimony of the impunity acquired by security and law enforcement agencies for custodial torture and extra judicial killings - acts which often extend across borders. In the extreme this has led to large scale displacement of communities, to their impoverisation and even confinement.

We need to break the legacy of hostility, and move forward towards making democracy, human rights and justice integral to the political economy of the region. We thank you for participating in these deliberations. Many of you have contributed to promoting peace and cooperation in South Asia. You have done this from a regional perspective and not from a narrow nationalistic lens. We hope that over the next two days, your deliberations will provide some insights on how to rebuild institutions and initiate action for post conflict settlements, for restoring rule of law and justice and ensuring human security – towards a sustainable peace.
