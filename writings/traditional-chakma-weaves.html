Traditional Chakma Weaves

A short descriptive note on back-strap loom (bain tant) weaving among Chittagong Hill Tracts communities, especially the Chakma. It covers the pinon sarong and its chabugi border, the khadi scarf, the alam design sampler and ritual cloths, and how spinning practices have changed. It notes that Adivasi women now sell their weaving in urban and export markets.

By Hameeda Hossain

In at least eleven ethnic communities living in the Chittagong Hill Tracts women continue to use the traditional back strap looms, known as bain tant, for weaving their sarongs and scarves, which are known by different names and the sarongs are of different lengths in each community,

Designs are differentiated by different communities. Lushai women’s sarong is woven with multi-colored, geometric designs on white. The Taungchengya sarong is woven with red, white and yellow stripes on a black warp. Chak women sarongs have long, horizontal stripes. (Dewan, 2002, p 9-12)

The Chakmas are the largest community in the Chittagong Hill Tracts. Traditionally, the sarong or pinon is woven in black with fine lines of dark blue and red bands, at the top and bottom. On one side of the pinon an intricate border known as sabugi or chabugi is woven in different colours. A multi coloured scarf known as khadi is worn across the chest.

Some of the designed cloths woven by Chakma women are used for ceremonial or ritual use. Young women are expected to weave a design sampler known as alam, with motifs from nature depicting flowers, birds, animals, tiger’s paws, snail’s tail, snake’s backbone. (Chakma, 2002, p. 4) Three old alams are on display at the Tribal Museum in Rangamati. The Tangon is woven as a ritual gift for the Buddhist temple. (Chakma, 2009) Cloths woven for Rajbon Bihar, or the New Year, are very artistically designed (Sattar, A., 1987, p. 38)

Previously ethnic women used to gin the cotton and roll it by hand onto a pech, before spinning it into yarn on a charka or spinning wheel. The weaver sits on the floor with legs stretched in front with the beam tied around her waist. Today they buy the yarn directly in the market and have succeeded in marketing their woven cloth in urban and export markets. Adhibashi women have maintained their weaving traditions and made them popular in the modern world.

References

Chakma M., and Zaman, N., Tradition of Backstrap Weaving in Bangladesh, Dhaka, 2009

Dewan, A., “Woven Textiles as Art: Examination of the Revival of Weaving in the Chittagong Hill Tracts”, Master’s Thesis at Concordia University, Montreal, 2002
