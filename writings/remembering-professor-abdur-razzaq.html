Remembering Professor Abdur Razzaq

A talk given at the Bengal Foundation on the display of Professor Abdur Razzaq's book collection. Dr. Hossain recalls how 'Sir' introduced her to Bengal's commerce and culture, urged her to learn Persian for her textile research, and connected her with nationalist and artistic circles. She stresses his tolerance of dissent, his legal challenge on teachers' political rights and his warnings about two-thirds majorities, and ends with quotations from him on discrimination.

By Hameeda Hossain

First published in Event at Bengal Foundation / Bengal Gallery marking Professor Razzaq's book collection (per text).

Professor Razaq’s portrait in the Bengal Gallery is a constant reminder of why he is so dear to us, and why his memory is so important for our society. Every time I see his portrait upon entering I wonder when we would see his books on display. Not only see the books but make use of them as he would have liked us to do. So let me congratulate Bengal Foundation and Litu for creating an intellectual island for all of us. Sir’s passion for books will be of great value to all of us.

Sir felt a great loss when many of his books were taken away in 1971. He used to visit the second hand bookstalls in New Market and actually found some again. When he visited us in Oxford in the eighties, he would go off every morning to search for archives and rare books. So this collection of books is a tribute to Sir’s learning and his search for knowledge.

Sir has had a long line of disciples and followers. Sardar Fazlul Karim has referred to him as his second father. I would like to say a few words about what Sir meant to me,

  • first in our personal relations;
  • second because he contributed to democratic practice and
  • lastly his valuable perceptions on state and governance.

When I came to Dhaka in 1962, it was Sir who introduced me to the intricacies of Bengal’s commerce and culture. He took me round to markets in the city and at ferry ghats and told me how food and cooking formed the social culture of a region. Sir knew many delicacies and liked to cook for us.

When I talked to him about my interest in studying Company records for condition of weavers in Bengal, he insisted that I should first learn Persian, because that was the state language of the Mughals. He brought out all his books on 16 and 17 centuries, travellers accounts, parliamentary records. He had himself read these and knew so much about this period. He was a political scientist, but his knowledge went beyond to history and philosophy.

I must acknowledge his contribution and of course that of Professor Anisuzaman to my thesis on textile production in Bengal.

Sir introduced me to a diverse circle of friends. There was the nationalist circle, whose discourses on democracy, education and many other subjects became known through New Values journal, through NACP pamphlets and through Forum. Writings by economists, lawyers, political scientists reflected current thinking in Bengal. Today, it is amazing to remember the diversity of this circle that allowed Marxists such a Badruddin Omar and even World Bank fellows to listen to each other. Sir was a patient listener and most tolerant of dissent. When I asked him why he did not interrupt in the debates he said it is always wiser to know what others think before a response. Another intimate circle of artist friends included Shilpacharjya Zainul Abedin, Mostafa Monwar and Kibria, who had led the renaissance in the Arts College. Sir was a central figure in many circles, political leaders would come to him for advice.

Sir belonged to several worlds, in academia, in art and amongst students. In the teachers’ common room in the university he used to spend a lot of time in discussions with students and colleagues. A favourite concern was the practice of political democracy and how to build an open society, which allows tolerance of diversity and dissent. An independent thinker he had a strong belief in democratic politics. But by this he meant open public participation and not limited to a collection of loyalists. When the Hamoodur Rahman Commission Report on University Reforms recommended that teachers not be associated in politics, Sir challenged this in the High Court and won his right. This happened around 1966-1968 when the University tried to control the students’ struggle.

The administration remained hostile and Sir resigned and left for an assignment in Balliol College Oxford.

Sir remained concerned with democratic practice and governance even after independence. He was afraid that an overwhelming majority of support would not form building bricks for democracy. In fact, he told us that before the elections in 1973 he had told political leaders that if they were to get a two third majority it would be a threat to democratic practice, which would allow people to participate in political debates, to express their views openly and to dissent. Without these conditions we would not have democracy in its real sense. We have not yet understood this advice.

Sir did not write much, but in a few tracts he expressed his important bearing on politics, on military rule and on development. I would like to end with a short quote from his talk on the state of the nation:

“No nation today is an island. Bangladesh and its nation is of course not. It is a link in a chain which is world wide. Economically, politically, in the world of technology and of ideas…”

His comment on development policies: “There is discrimination between men and women, there is discrimination between urban and rural areas, there is discrimination between rich and poor. …the stamp of the policy decision unmistakably is divisive and discriminatory…the net effect is the encouragement of two nations within the nation – those who have and those who have not profited from public enterprise.”

He ends in a note of pessimism which we cannot afford to forget.
