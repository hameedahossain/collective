Recollections of Kormojibi Nari

Recalls Dr. Hossain's association with Shirin Akhtar and the workers' rights organisation Kormojibi Nari since it formed in 1991. It describes their joint work with ASK on lay-offs, garment workers' rights, the Yasmin and Shima custodial rape cases, factory disasters (Spectrum, KTS), minimum wage campaigns and organising agricultural workers. It ends by urging KN to stay with the workers and not be swayed by political loyalties.

By Hameeda HossainMay 2011

Written for the Kormojibi Nari anniversary, May 2011.

My association with Kormojibi Nari came about as a result of my acquaintance with Shirin Akhtar in the eighties. Many of us had been involved with the movement against General Ershad’s military rule, in the hope that our struggle would lead to a more democratic system. As the government became more repressive in the latter part of the eighties, we tried to monitor and investigate police violence to silence voices of dissent. Our report entitled “Lawless Law Enforcement” showed how the rule of law had been violated under orders from the military president.

But the difference between us was that Shirin had herself been a victim of state violence. As a leader of the Jatiyo Samajtantrik League she had been arrested under General Ziaur Rahman’s rule and detained for several months in the Rangpur and Dhaka Central jails. Upon her release she became involved in the party’s trade union activities and through this reached out to workers. In the mid-eighties, I had joined several friends and political/social activists in forming Ain o Salish Kendra, (a legal aid organization) because we were convinced that electoral democracy, as it was practiced in Bangladesh, would merely accentuate the aggrandizing power of political leaders unless citizens themselves could create and expand their access to justice. Our paths were thus different in mode but not in objectives. While political parties engaged in a struggle for power, we could merely support the struggles of ordinary people to assert their rights as citizens, at the workplace, in the community and within the family. Nevertheless, all of us, whether we belonged to a political party or were outside, were part of the struggle for democracy – in the expectation that a return to civilian rule would widen the space for political participation, for freedom and for enabling the realization of social and economic rights.

It was in 1991 that Shirin Akhtar, with a group of her colleagues, joined a large coalition of women’s groups in commemorating the International Women’s Day on 8 March. As women’s rallies around Dhaka reminded us of the beginnings of the women textile workers’ movement in Chicago in 1857 for equal wages, of Clara Zetkin’s call for an international movement for women’s rights, they must have inspired Shirin to recognize that the inequities of class and gender were not necessarily reflected in the national struggle. So the group began to engage more deliberately with women workers and by May 1991 registered themselves under the name of Kormojibi Nari, and asserted the principle that all women be identified as workers, whether their labour was recognized in the formal sector, unrecognized in the agriculture and informal sectors, or invisible in the domestic space.

At this time the World Bank’s structural adjustment programs were being critiqued for their negative effects on the economy and for aggravating unemployment and poverty. In Bangladesh workers began to face industrial pay cuts, lay offs and closures. Both Ain o Salish Kendra and Kormojibi Nari decided to investigate the effect of these lay offs on women workers in the AB Biscuit Factory in Tongi. The findings revealed a heart rending story of how women workers had to cope with the impact of these economic policies and pressures. Kormojibi Nari used this study not only to campaign against closures, but also to enable workers to set up alternative means of income by training them in different skills.

Since the export garment industry employed the largest number of women it made sense for Kormojibi Nari to start working with garment workers: telling them about their rights, under the labour laws, under ILO Conventions; by bringing their problems into the trade union discourse, by supporting their strikes, raising a voice against their termination, even directing their legal cases to organizations such as Ain o Salish Kendra. In 2005, when a Kormojibi Nari staff found a garment worker, lying almost in a coma in Savar, she took the initiative to alert the organization, so that she could be taken to the One Stop Crisis Center in the Dhaka Medical College Hospital. She had been raped and left under a bridge. KN’s support was instrumental in drawing attention to the insecurity of garment workers. To involve the workers in their movements KN decided to start workers’ cells in some garment factories, so as to build solidarity amongst them and to link them to the larger trade union movement. Even though not many women workers participated in trade union activities, through the efforts of KN, their demands for child crèches and maternity leave has indeed been taken up by the leading workers’ federation.

In 1995, an incident involving rape and murder by the police of a young domestic worker Yasmin raised protests around the country not only amongst women’s groups, but by many others, particularly as the government tried to deny allegations against the four policemen. As a result of a collective struggle, in which KN played a leading role, the High Court in 2005 gave a verdict of guilty against the four accused police. However, in a subsequent case in which Shima another garment worker was alleged to have been raped and been found dead in jail custody, the accused were exonerated on the grounds of insufficient evidence. Members of the political party which had protested against Yasmin’s death while their party was in opposition, were reluctant to protest in Shima’s case, because their party was now in power. Nevertheless, it goes to KN’s credit, that they kept their politics aside, and joined the struggle for a fair trial.

Industrial safety is a very important issue for workers and their families. In 2005 over 60 workers were killed when Spectrum Garment Factory collapsed in Polashbari, Savar. In the next year, a fire in KTS factory in Chittagong again led to many burn deaths and serious injuries. Both Shirin and I went to the site, visited the patients in Chittagong to ensure that the garment factory was responsible for their treatment, and then procured oxygen and other necessary equipment for their use in the Burn Unit of the Dhaka Medical College Hospital. Several organizations worked closely with some trade unions to ensure that the management and BGMEA meet their obligations to provide compensation and treatment of workers. Jointly, several organizations including KN and ASK filed cases holding the management and several regulatory institutions liable. The slow progress in the courts and the economic power of the manufacturers association has been obstructive to the course of justice. In order to ensure that safety and health conditions are observed, about 15 organisations came together to form an informal network known as Sramik Nirapotta Forum. KN along with ASK, BLAST, Nijera Kori, BILS has played a leading role in promoting compliance with safety and health measures and other labour laws. In doing so, the pressure for safety and health measures has also been taken up by citizens’ groups overseas and some buyers have set up compliance standards.

Conditions of women garment workers have obviously improved some since the first factories were set up in 1986, but their wages are far from being equivalent to a basic living wage, conditions in many factories do not guarantee safety or health, and workers are exposed to sexual and other forms of harassment, both at work and in public. Their right to life and freedom of movement is thus often under threat. KN has taken up these issues, by supporting the workers’ demand for a living wage, and participating in the negotiations with the Minimum Wage Board. As a result of pressure from the trade unions, the minimum wages were amended after 12 years in 2006, and again in 2010. Although the announcement of Tk 3000.0 was far from the workers’ demand of Tk 5000.0, several trade union representatives signed the agreement. Workers were not placated and their strikes were suppressed with force, several leading trade unionists were arrested and bail was given after repeated applications to the High Court.

More recently KN has branched into the informal sector, trying to organize women agricultural labour. Since 2008 it has worked in 30 unions in 18 upazillas. KN proposes to organize women agricultural workers, to establish a minimum wage and to assist in formulating a law for agricultural work.

The struggle for workers’ rights is a challenge in a society, in which inequalities have escalated, leaving the rich and powerful dominant in decision making positions -- from the large number of business persons in parliament to their nomination in advisory and decision making positions.

To meet this challenge is not an easy task, and the struggle of women workers to organize themselves will need to be supported by women’s movements, as well as by trade unions. Their interaction with multiple actors will convince them of their rights so that they take up their case as an issue of justice and fairness. We can collectively support them to resist the controls imposed by the powerful. Kormojibi Nari can play an important role in taking this struggle forward, if it stays with the workers and is not swayed by political loyalties.
