Welcome Address: SAHR Meeting, Kathmandu

By Hameeda Hossainc. late 2010
South Asians for Human Rights (SAHR) members' meeting, Kathmandu.

Let me welcome all SAHR members. In particular, I would like to thank Justice Upadhaya for joining us today. Some of us present today met in Neemrana Fort in 2000 and crafted a declaration that would guide South Asians for Human Rights in bringing together human rights defenders in the region. Mr Gujral, the chairperson of SAHR, has been unable to join us because of his health. I would however like to place on record that inspite of his indisposition he has continued to inspire and counsel us.

Ten years on, many new faces have joined us, which indicates not only the strength of the human rights movement in South Asia, but reflects the challenges presented by the wide range of violations of human rights by both state and non-state actors.

We join with all other human rights defenders to congratulate Aung San Suu Kyi for overcoming 27 years in solitary confinement. We see hope in her renewed will to struggle for the release of many others still in jail and we support her call for an end to the rule of the generals in Myanmar. Her release might have come sooner if governments in the region, as indeed elsewhere, had stood firm in demanding justice and democracy, and not been tempted into negotiating with the generals for development of natural resources.

Many of us have engaged in promoting human rights at the grass roots within our own countries but felt it necessary to synergise our struggles at the regional and international level. This is because we share a common vision for our societies in South Asia to make freedom and development meaningful for all citizens, irrespective of diversity in ethnicity, religion, sect, class, caste and gender. The challenge that a regional network like ours faces is to reinforce our national struggles, to build just and humane societies. SAHR therefore has tried to evolve complementary and supportive approaches to promote substantive equality, genuine citizens’ participation in public decision making and to strengthen democratic institutions.

While SAHR recognizes the primary responsibility of the state to implement the rights guaranteed in our constitutions and in conformity with international and regional human rights standards, we believe that this is not possible without an active engagement by conscious citizens. It is through the convergence of formal rules and informal social norms that we can evolve a human rights culture. We believe that the imperatives of a vibrant and just society lie in the establishment of peace, democracy and human rights.

In the next few minutes I will try to highlight a few areas that SAHR has focused on.

Progress reports on South Asia project its investment potential and economic achievements: double digit growth, the micro-credit miracle and increasing prosperity. But this cannot gloss over the reality of poverty and injustice in our societies: of children at work when they should be in school, slums evicted so cities become the playground of the rich; of police raids, arrests and disappearances, and endemic violence.

The challenges to peace continue to be daunting: as war or protracted, low intensity conflicts endanger our security and freedom. In Afghanistan and Pakistan, villages have been wiped out by drones, while innocent civilians have become victims of suicide bombers or armed attacks on mosques. Military intervention has brought an end to war in Sri Lanka, but will a majority dictated post conflict settlement sustain peace? Within each of our countries militarized solutions have played havoc with people’s lives in Sri Lanka, Baluchistan, in Kashmir, in the North East and in the Chittagong Hill Tracts. The rule of law has been undermined while peace accords still await implementation.

Going on for 60 years hostile relations between India and Pakistan have been a major threat to peace in the region. Government officials were reluctantly pushed into a series of interim dialogues and composite dialogues, which never reached a conclusion. Each time solutions have been aborted. After the Mumbai incident, citizens’ protests prevented the hysteria from descending into another armed fiasco. Spontaneously, SAHR Pakistan and SAHR India arranged exchange visits by human rights and cultural activists and media leaders (in cooperation with SAFMA). They met with leaders of political parties, government officials, media, cultural activists to demand an end to war talk. Other voices too joined in the peace efforts, which led to a de-escalation. It is time for citizens to start their dialogues for peace.

Unresolved conflicts between the two countries have provided a breeding ground for extremist violence. Peripatetic armed groups have multiplied under the cover of Jihad and spread their tentacles from Afghanistan to Kashmir, to Bangladesh and perhaps other locations. Whether these are grounded in ideology, nourished by national rivalries or narrow power interests, they need to be exposed. Investigations by SAHR have revealed the background, affiliations and regional connections of the groups responsible for wanton violence against civilians. It showed the need for a collective, regional approach to address the growing militancy of obscurantist groups. The states of South Asia need to seek regional solutions for peace in Afghanistan, in Kashmir and elsewhere rather than to resort to narrowly conceived national ambitions.

The emergence of terrorists is often used by governments to justify methods of lawless law enforcement. Promulgation of anti-terrorist laws in each of our state, whether at the behest of foreign powers after 2001, or compelled by internal political dialectics have resulted in violations of the right to life, liberty and freedom from torture. In all our countries, the impunity for security forces has allowed for extra-judicial killings, custodial torture, disappearances. SAHR undertook a comparative study of laws and practices in Bangladesh, India, Pakistan and Sri Lanka. This demands an objective examination of our security regimes, because of the threat to constitutional supremacy and human freedoms. This morning when discussing the findings of this study we should also consider means to support each other in challenging practices that threaten democratic values and human rights.

The right to equality under the law has been enshrined in all our constitutions. It is an essential ingredient of democracy that all citizens be treated equally irrespective of religion, ethnicity, caste, sect and gender. In our electoral democracy, however, majoritarian interests have served to create and reinforce communal differences and more recently these differences have been used by political ideologues to fuel discrimination, leading to internecine, communal conflicts. In 2007, SAHR set up a South Asian People’s Commission for Minorities, whose members visited four countries. Their conclusions published under the title of Including the Excluded gave evidence of how ethnic and religious exclusion in each country fed into the political dialectics of the neighbouring country because a minority in one country was a majority in the other. This report was followed by a more recent study on the use of religion by political establishments to discriminate and exclude communities within a nation state. The authors of these reports will present their findings tomorrow, which should lead to intensive discussions on how our competitive electoral systems have conditioned our understanding of citizenship.

Competitive claims over land by the powerful and appropriation of natural resources have led to major internal displacements. The most brazen example being recent post war internment and displacement of Tamils. SAHR’s research found that while vast numbers were displaced and remained in poverty in Baluchistan, the land’s resources of gas and minerals were used for development of affluent sections of urban Pakistan. The Minority Commission report in Chittagong Hill Tracts reveals that seizure of land and forest resources has excluded the indigenous communities from their lands.

Because of the link between governance and human rights or constitutional rights, SAHR decided to assess and monitor the behaviour of democratic institutions. Our review of electoral systems prompted the formulation of recommendations for reform of electoral laws and mechanisms which were taken up by a few of the election commissions in the region. Subsequently SAHR has started a program of parliament watch which is ongoing, to assess parliaments’ effectiveness as legislators and as monitors of executive behaviour. The findings from several of the countries presented at one of the sessions tomorrow should stimulate critical debate on the weaknesses of the prevailing systems and yield insights on how citizens’ activism could act as a restraint on political excesses.

The struggle for human rights is an ongoing one, and cannot be limited to round table discussions. Ten years of working together across South Asia has shown us the formidable and complex challenges of realizing human rights. We need to share our experiences and knowledge in devising strategies and action programs. But above all we can derive confidence and hope from the ongoing struggles of all those who refuse to surrender to injustice, oppression and discrimination or disasters – man made or natural. This should sustain us in the next phase of our work.

Let us inspire ourselves with the words of Aung San Suu Kyi:

“Á revolution which aims merely at changing official policies and institutions with a view to an improvement in material conditions has little chance of genuine success. Without a revolution of the spirit, the forces which produced the iniquities of the old order would continue to be operative, posing a constant threat to the process of reform and regeneration. It is not enough merely to call for freedom, democracy and human rights. There has to be a united determination to persevere in the struggle, to resist the corrupting influences of desire, ill will, ignorance and fear.”
